# Context-aware message formatting

Use this skill when drafting, revising, or specifying a human-facing message or recurring report for a chat or messaging channel. It is especially useful when the source data is technical, operational, metric-heavy, or generated by an agent and must be made easy to scan on a phone.

This skill defines formatting at the prompt/content layer. Do not assume that the delivery channel will add spacing, bold labels, icons, or visual structure automatically.

## Goals

- Make the useful point obvious in the first line.
- Let a human scan the message quickly without reconstructing a log or tool result.
- Preserve the facts, caveats, uncertainty, and required action.
- Match the channel, recipient, language, relationship, and urgency.
- Use visual cues—including emoji when appropriate—as semantic signals, not decoration.
- Keep the format stable enough for recurring reports while allowing content-sensitive variation.

## Workflow

1. **Identify the context**
   - Channel and rendering rules: WhatsApp, Telegram, Slack, SMS, email, or another surface.
   - Recipient and relationship: private human, group, customer, operator, or internal team.
   - Purpose: ordinary update, alert, request, correction, handoff, guest message, or recurring report.
   - Urgency and risk: routine, uncertain, operationally important, safety-sensitive, financial/legal, or urgent.
   - Language, tone, and expected reading device; assume phone reading for chat unless known otherwise.

2. **Inspect an established good example when available**
   - Prefer an existing delivered report or approved template from the same channel and audience.
   - Identify its stable visual grammar: title marker, section labels, blank-line rhythm, metric bullets, status symbols, and closing line.
   - Reuse the grammar, not unrelated factual wording or private data.

3. **Separate invariants from adaptive choices**
   - Invariants belong in the prompt contract: required facts, ordering, privacy rules, final action/next step, and channel restrictions.
   - Adaptive choices belong to the content: section names, level of detail, language, status, and emoji selection.
   - Do not turn illustrative examples into a rigid universal template unless the channel or user explicitly requires exact output.

4. **Write a prompt-level format contract**
   Tell the generating agent what the final message must look like. Specify:
   - whether it must return only the message;
   - the title and section order;
   - short-line and blank-line requirements;
   - permitted channel-native formatting such as single-asterisk bold labels;
   - whether tables, Markdown headings, raw logs, internal identifiers, or technical codes are prohibited;
   - how to express missing data, uncertainty, urgency, and the next step;
   - the emoji policy described below.

5. **Run the recipient test**
   Read the result as the recipient on the target channel. Remove duplicated caveats, semicolon-heavy metric dumps, raw tool language, unexplained error codes, internal paths, and details that do not change the recipient's understanding or action.

## Recommended message structure

Choose the smallest structure that fully serves the purpose.

### Ordinary update

- Opening or title.
- What happened / what matters.
- Relevant context or impact.
- Next step or question.

### Alert or incident

Use plain language and cover, in a visible order:

1. What happened.
2. Impact or safety consequence.
3. Action already taken.
4. Retry, next step, and whether the recipient must act.

Translate technical errors into human meaning. Do not send a bare error code or raw log excerpt.

### Recurring status/report

A useful phone-friendly pattern is:

- title on its own line;
- status and time/window;
- compact activity or metric bullets;
- findings grouped by severity or topic;
- source/coverage caveat when relevant;
- clear next step or “no action needed” line;
- final safety/read-only/coverage statement when required.

Use section labels such as `*Status*`, `*Activity*`, `*Findings*`, and `*Next step*`; adapt or replace them when the audience or subject calls for it.

## Context-aware emoji policy

Emoji selection should be dynamic and content-sensitive. Do not hard-code one emoji into every report, and do not force a universal whitelist that prevents a natural fit.

Choose emoji based on:

- **Message purpose:** a chart/report marker for a dashboard, a greeting marker for a welcome, a warning marker for an alert, or a directional/action marker for a next step.
- **Content state:** calm/healthy, successful, uncertain, degraded, blocked, urgent, unavailable, or awaiting action.
- **Audience and relationship:** professional, friendly, guest-facing, internal operations, or sensitive/private.
- **Language and cultural fit:** use symbols that are broadly understandable and do not create an unintended tone.
- **Channel rendering:** prefer symbols that render reliably on the target channel and avoid visually noisy combinations.

Use a small number of meaningful cues:

- A title marker can help recurring reports (`📊`, `📈`, `🗓️`, or another context-appropriate symbol).
- A status symbol can communicate the current state (`🟢`, `🟠`, `🔴`, `⚪`, or a better-fitting symbol when context calls for it).
- A section symbol can improve scanning when the report has several sections (`📥`, `🛡️`, `🤖`, `📚`, `🧠`, `➡️`, or another semantically appropriate symbol).
- Do not put an emoji on every bullet, repeat the same symbol mechanically, or add decorative emoji that compete with the message.
- Use no emoji when the audience, topic, channel, accessibility needs, or sensitivity make plain text clearer.
- For urgent or safety-relevant content, the wording must carry the urgency; emoji may reinforce it but must never be the only warning.

A prompt may provide examples such as:

```text
📊 *Report title*

*Status*
🟢 COMPLETE — no action needed

📥 *Activity*
• New items: 0
• Affected areas: 0

➡️ *Next step*
No action required.
```

Treat these symbols as examples of a visual grammar, not mandatory output. Select or omit them according to the actual message content and context.

## Channel formatting rules

- Use the native formatting that the channel reliably supports.
- For WhatsApp-like chat, prefer short paragraphs, blank lines, `*bold labels*`, and `•` bullets.
- Do not use Markdown tables in chat messages.
- Avoid formal Markdown headings (`#`) in messages intended for chat.
- Keep links intact; put important links on their own line when that improves scanning.
- Keep timestamps and metric values on one readable line where possible.
- Do not expose private phone numbers, email addresses, credentials, access codes, internal paths, approval tokens, raw logs, or unrelated personal information.
- Do not mention internal tool names, agent mechanics, or implementation details unless the recipient needs them.
- Avoid all-caps emphasis, excessive exclamation marks, decorative separators, and over-bolded text.

## Prompt contract template

Adapt this block to the actual task rather than copying it blindly:

```text
Return only one concise [channel]-friendly message.

Use the following visual structure:
- title on its own line;
- blank lines between short sections;
- native bold labels and compact bullets;
- no tables, raw logs, internal identifiers, or unexplained error codes.

Choose visual cues dynamically from the message purpose, audience, urgency, and current result. Use a meaningful title/status/section emoji only when it improves scanning; do not decorate every line and do not let emoji replace plain-language urgency.

Keep the main point first. State missing or uncertain metrics explicitly. End with the concrete next step, action required, or no-action status.
```

For recurring reports, add the exact required fields and exact mandatory closing text below the contract, while keeping the visual cues adaptive.

## Quality gate

Before presenting or sending the message, verify:

- The recipient, purpose, and urgency are clear.
- The first line communicates the point or identifies the report.
- Facts, dates, times, links, names, and statuses are accurate.
- Every required metric or caveat is present, and unavailable values are not guessed.
- The message is readable on a phone: short lines, spacing, and compact bullets.
- The visual hierarchy is consistent without becoming repetitive.
- Emoji, if used, fit the content and audience, are not overused, and do not obscure risk or action.
- No tables, raw logs, secrets, internal identifiers, or unnecessary private details are exposed.
- Alerts explain what happened, impact/safety, action taken, and next step.
- The final action, question, or no-action expectation is unambiguous.

If the user or channel requires an exact format, follow that requirement over these defaults. If the task involves external delivery, prepare the message carefully and respect the applicable approval/safety gate; never send a half-finished message.
